# Treatment and cast

The treatment is the contract for every keyframe, clip and string. Keep it to one page.

## Template

```markdown
# "<Song title>" (The Roast of <first name>): treatment v1, <date>

One paragraph: format, length (from the measured song), premise, who roasts.

## Rules that can't bend
- <Subject> is cool and having fun. Laughs hardest at their own roast.
  Never sweaty, sad, humiliated or a body joke. <their own look notes>
- Binding changes: review/rulings.md overrides this file.
- No names of the employer, its products, customers or colleagues. No real logos.
- No other real people. Everyone else is a robot or an object.
- No model-drawn text. Every sign, card, screen and receipt is generated blank.
- Every cast member has a different silhouette, material and colour.
- Real animation: one clear action per character per generated shot.
- Not photoreal. No celebrity lookalikes. No religious symbols.

## Look
Style in one sentence. Setting. Palette table (role | hex). Fonts (role | font).

## Cast
| Name | What it is | Silhouette and colour |

## Sections
| Section | Who sings | Where | What we see |

## Spine device
One prop that grows through the film and pays off at the end.

## On-screen text (all drawn in code)
Captions, hook words, static tally cards or tags, quote cards with dates, name cards, end card.

## Formats and budget
Resolution, fps, aspect, cap, plan, reserve, providers per role.
```

## The subject

- Ask how they want to look and what they reject. Write it down; it overrides your taste.
- Do not state age, weight or anything not given. Use photos only if the subject supplied them for likeness, and keep them out of the repo and the output.
- The subject reacts on camera regularly (waves, conducts, laughs). Plan a reaction shot at least every 15 seconds, because the roast only works if we see them enjoying it.

## The cast

Roasters are the subject's AI helpers, tools and habits made into characters. Each one gets:
- a role in the roast (MC, the agents, the permission prompt, the printer that keeps the tally);
- a silhouette nobody else has (tall and thin, squat, flat, round, faceted);
- its own material and colour;
- one signature action it can do in four seconds.

Fictional example (subject "Sam, a product manager"):

| Name | What it is | Silhouette and colour |
|---|---|---|
| Sam | guest of honour | relaxed in a big green armchair, oversized headphones round the neck, always grinning |
| Gavel | the MC | a tall brass judge's gavel robot in a purple jacket |
| The Drafts | seven agents | seven paper-sheet robots, each a different size and colour, each smaller than the last |
| Ping | the notification | a round red badge with stubby legs and a bell |
| Ruler | the editor | a long wooden ruler that folds itself in half |

No two characters share a body plan. When a gag needs "two of the same", make the second one visibly different (smaller, another colour, another head), because image-to-video models blur identical characters into clones.

## Setting and look

Pick a look that suits the genre and is not photoreal, so nobody mistakes a gag for footage: bold 2D cartoon, cut paper, claymation-like, a 1970s variety show, a nature documentary painting. Keep three to five settings at most; each extra setting is more keyframes to keep consistent.

Gags about a work product go in a generic place (a plain rehearsal room, a model on a table), never a recognisable office, interface or product.

## Spine device

A single prop that grows: a tally receipt, a stack of drafts, a to-do list that becomes a carpet. It gives the choruses a physical escalation instead of three identical chants, and gives the ending a payoff.

## End card

Claim only what is true: "Based on <first name>'s real AI chat history. Some scenes rebuilt for comedy." Never "every joke is true" unless a reviewer has checked every single one. Never show transcripts, file paths or dossier pages.
